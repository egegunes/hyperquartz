I've been very excited since I learned about Tetsu Kesuya's [new 10-pours v60 recipe](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=k0nsShguOsU). Although the video was published 5 months ago, the Instagram algorithm recently started bombarding me with content about this recipe. Naturally, I gave it a try and the results were so amazing that I was talking about it with anyone who's even remotely into coffee. To my surprise, no one I've talked to has ever heard of it. We have to change that!

My go-to recipe for v60 was James Hoffmann's [ultimate v60 technique](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AI4ynXzkSQo) for years. I perfected my pouring technique and the timing over the years, so results were pretty consistent throughout brews. The only thing I needed to tweak was the grind size according to the beans I use. From time to time, baristas were recommending different recipes to me, but I was never intrigued enough to try them out: 3 pours instead of 2 or bloom for 30 seconds instead of 45, so what?

What intrigued me about Kesuya's method was how truly contrarian it is. It contradicts everything that coffee aficionados were repeating for the last 10 years: "go finer", "limit the contact time", "max 3 pours or it'll be overextracted"...  First, Tetsu goes ridiculously coarse with 40-45 clicks on Comandante. 40 clicks! I don't think anyone has ever brewed coffee ground this coarse before him. Second, he maximizes the contact time by doing 10 separate pours. Crazy.

Here's the recipe for reference:
- 1:15 ratio, which means we'll use 20 grams of coffee and 300 grams of water.
- Bloom with 30 grams of water for 30 seconds.
- Pour 30 grams of water every 15 seconds after blooming.

This is it. The whole brew finishes in under 3 minutes (Tetsu says it might take 3.5 minutes depending on beans and the quality of your grinder).

What I like most in coffee is body. I don't enjoy those tea-like clean cups. I want texture. I want mouthfeel. This recipe maximizes that. In Tetsu's words, the coffee feels syrupy; in mine, it's velvety. What's amazing is you get that without sacrificing the sweetness. The cup is low in acidity and high in sweetness and body. Honestly, this was a combination that I thought was almost impossible to achieve.

I really like how Tetsu talks about the idea behind this recipe (slightly paraphrased):
> The trend in brewing was to focus on avoiding negative flavors in the cup. But aren't you also losing positive flavors? **What makes coffee delicious is not the absence of negatives. It's the presence of positives.**

Over the last few days I used this recipe with the Colombian beans I had at home. I wouldn't describe those beans as interesting or even complex—they were just okay. With this recipe I got results so amazing that I now want to apply this to all the beans I enjoyed in the past.

Of course, I wouldn't be writing all this if the method didn't pass the ultimate test: my wife. While I can tolerate unpleasant tastes just for the sake of caffeine intake, she has absolutely no tolerance for bitter or sour tastes when it comes to black filter coffee. This morning I brewed for two of us by scaling up the recipe to accommodate two cups. Keeping the same ratio, I used 40 grams of coffee and 600 grams of water. This meant pouring 60 grams of water every 15 seconds. I was worried that my v60-02 might overflow, so I ground the coffee with 42 clicks instead of 40 to ensure the water drains faster. The method passed the test with flying colors! At first she said the coffee tasted strange, like very fruity, and throughout the cup she complimented it twice. 

I honestly think this method is one of the biggest innovations that happened in coffee in the last few years. I strongly urge everyone who reads this post to give it a try, and if you do, send me an email to tell me about your experience!
