Mostly learned from hours at the wheel and in the studio. A collection of observations, advice, and notes, some of which you may find to generalize beyond pottery.

- clay and tools
	- different properties of the clay means your technique needs to change
		- porcelain needs less water, you throw with slip instead of water
	- preparing the clay well matters otherwise you're fighting a losing battle from the onset
		- air bubbles, uneven reclaim consistency, wedging direction (clockwise or counterclockwise should generally match wheel direction)
	- clay can get tired if you throw and reclaim it too many times
		- you lose the tiny particles that give it plasticity
		- recycle throwing water and throwing slip
		- ironically, mold is not always bad
			- it helps breakdown clay particles and returns some of the plasticity to tired clay
	- tools should be used to help you make the parts you enjoy about pottery more reliably enjoyable
		- a good tungsten carbide trimming tool that wont rust and stays sharp, a good set of bats that aren't warped
		- also an argument *not* to use some tools
			- e.g. giffin grip -- i dont use it because to me centering is part of the pottery process and removing that makes the act of making a piece feel not entirely my own
- routine and showing up
	- routine builds community -- it is a third space
		- show up at the same time and same place continuously and the people who also do become familiar faces
	- [[thoughts/50 pounds of pots|50 pounds of pots]]
		- volume matters
		- and you can only achieve volume by showing up
	- over time it deepens your perception of ceramics all around you
		- you lift every plate and mug at every restaurant
		- seek out tea shops and ceramics stores everywhere you visit
		- admire a good pour from a cup or a teapot
	- you will have bad throwing days and thats ok
		- progression will not be linear
			- look at your progress in terms of months not single pieces
		- if it happens consistently, start looking at your environment and yourself
			- are your materials good?
			- are your tools good? do they need to be replaced?
			- are you feeling good?
				- are you rested? fed? hydrated?
				- the mental also affects the body
					- being stressed or upset at something rarely leads me to having a good session
					- the act of crossing the boundary into the studio helps my brain to leave those things temporarily behind -- this is studio time
- on being attached to your work
	- everything can be saved but it's not always worth the effort to
		- wall becoming too thing when pulling, bisque fix, reglazing
		- learning to kill you darlings and try again
			- see volume argument above
	- despite your best efforts, you're not going to know what a piece looks like until you fire it
		- does not help that unfired glaze looks nothing like its finished thing
		- small variations in glaze mixtures, kiln placement, humidity, clay body, etc. all matter
- technique
	- there are step function changes in forms that require changes in technique to throw properly
		- bowls after a certain size centrifugal force matters
		- after a certain thinness on curved forms, curvature matters a lot to keep it stable
		- cylinders after a certain height you can no longer couple your hands so you need to develop better proprioception
			- after even more, you cannot even see your hands as they are inside the cylinder
	- there is no 'right' way to do anything
		- everyone has different bodies and different goals for what they are trying to make. you can take inspiration from others but ultimately find what works for your body.
	- you can't learn everything just by watching
		- so much tactility / fluency is learned by doing and getting reps in
	- getting better at pottery is less about getting stronger to control the clay better but more about the tactile fluency with the material itself and with your own body
		- being in dialogue with the clay, knowing when you can push it further, when its tired, knowing how much clay you have left in the walls, hearing the sound of clay thats just about too thin to trim any more
			- cant collar too aggressively, cant belly out too aggressively, cant pull too aggressively
		- being in dialogue with your body, knowing where your hands are when you can't see them, knowing if your elbow is pointed up to execute a straight pull, knowing how much pressure to apply if at all

### Appendix

- *wheel*: the pottery wheel.
- *clay body*: the type of clay you are using. Porcelain, stoneware, etc. are all different types of clay bodies. Can also refer to a specific type of clay (e.g. 550 English Porcelain)
- *slip*: a mixture of water and fine clay particles. Usually produced when *throwing* and can be used to lubricate the clay so your fingers don't catch on the clay body. Some also use slip as a decorative layer on top of their pieces to create texture.
- *reclaim*: recycled clay. Usually done by dumping all your *trimming* scraps, *throwing* water, *slip*, etc. into a bucket and letting it slake down into a homogenous mixture. This can then be reclaimed on a plaster board and *wedged* back into usable clay again.
- *wedging*: the act of processing the clay manually with your hands to align clay particles and remove air bubbles. Usually done via spiral wedging, ram's head wedging, or cut wedging.
- *throwing*: putting clay on the *wheel* and making something.
	- *centering*: stabilizing the clay so there is no wobbling when you spin the *wheel*.
	- *pulling*: making the form taller by raising up clay from the base or thinning out the walls.
	- *collaring*: making a part of the form narrower.
	- *bellying out*: making a part of the form wider.
- *greenware*: pottery pieces that have just been *thrown*.
- *leather-hard*: *greenware* pieces that have been left to dry a bit, enough for the surface of the clay to feel like cold leather. It shouldn't deform to the touch but still holds a bit of malleability.
- *trimming*: the act of putting a *leather-hard* piece on the wheel again and using tools to pare down and refine the form. Usually thinning out walls that couldn't be made thinner in the *throwing* process and making a foot ring.
- *bone-dry*: *leather-hard* pieces that have been left out enough to become dry as bone. Usually at this stage the clay has visibly turned a lighter color due to the lack of water in the body. Clay at this stage is incredibly fragile and should be handled with care as it can break easily.
- *bisque firing*: the act of putting *bone-dry* pieces into a kiln. Inside the kiln, the temperatures are hot enough (around *cone 4*) to chemically alter the body. This hardens the clay substantially and makes the body porous and ready to *glaze*.
- *glazing*: the act of putting glaze on *bisque*, usually either by painting or dipping. Glaze is usually a mixture of clay, water, and various impurities that will result in interesting chemical reactions that give pieces distinct colors, patterns, and variation.
- *glaze firing*: the act of putting *glazed* pieces into a kiln. This firing is hot enough to cause the chemical reactions that turn glaze into glass (vitrification).
- *cone*: a system for measuring heatwork which is a combination of temperature and time. High-fire usually is around *cone 10* which is a kiln temperature of around 1305 degrees celsius and is where full vitrification happens and pieces become food-safe. Mid-fire usually is around *cone 6* which is an approximate kiln temperature of 1222 degrees celsius.
