New York City offers whatever you want, quickly and without much fuss. 

## Whitney Museum

We visited Whitney Museum first on Halloween eve. The museum was open until 10 pm and entrance was free. We didn't miss this chance of course.

We started directly from the 5th floor, top of the museum. The floor was dedicated to artist [Minnie Evans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minnie_Evans) whom I've never heard before. Her mandala-like paintings have a lot of Ancient American motifs, so I thought she's Mexican or Peruvian or maybe a Native American. After walking around the floor my wife, Gule, whispered to me "I think Evans is a black woman". I couldn't understand how she intuited that, but it was true:

Jay Defeo's *The Rose* might be the most impressive piece of art in all New York. Its scale, the center and the cracks on the concrete... I don't remember having a somatic reaction this intense in front of an artwork. I was getting sucked into it. I think I spent at least 10 minutes looking at it and mesmerized.

## MoMA

Our next stop was The Museum of Modern Art, the famous MoMA. I knew it has a good collection of Magritte, Rothko, Pollock and Matisse. So this was the museum I was most excited about.

I don't know what's about it but seeing The Lovers by Magritte in real life was even more impressive. This surprised me because I looked at this piece maybe dozens of times. Usually artworks I haven't known about affect me more. Some artworks move you in some way when you share the same physical space with them, like how I felt looking at Defeo's Rose at Whitney. 

<figure>
<img src="https://hypersubject.b-cdn.net/images/whitney/magritte-the-lovers.jpg" />
<figcaption>René Magritte, The Lovers, 1928</figcaption>
</figure>

Walking around the floor, I noticed a long line of people. They were waiting for their turn to take a photo of Van Gogh's Starry Night. Having visited the Van Gogh Museum in Amsterdam and honestly not being that impressed with his paintings, I didn't care much about it before visiting the MoMA. Since I had no intention of photographing a painting that is probably photographed already a bazillion times, I started looking at the Starry Night from the side. I couldn't believe my eyes. I could swear the light around the stars and the swirls were moving, rotating, vibrating...


<figure>
<img src="https://hypersubject.b-cdn.net/images/whitney/van-gogh-starry-night.jpg" />
<figcaption>Vincent van Gogh, The Starry Night, 1889</figcaption>
</figure>

Being fascinated by two paintings that I had digitally seen multiple times encouraged me even more to see Rothko and Pollock's works which both I'm not fond of.

Especially about Mark Rothko, even though I never get why people make such a fuss about him, I suspended my judgement until I see one of his paintings. People were saying how his paintings affected them when they experience them with their true scale in three dimensions.

I saw seven of Rothko's paintings: No. 1, No. 10, No. 5/No. 22, No. 5/No. 24, No. 37/No. 19, Untitled (Black on Gray). They didn't do much for me. I'm not an art critic. I'm not well versed in art history either. So you can easily say "ege, you simply didn't understand what you saw!" and you would probably be right. However I would respond by asking why didn't the same ignorance of mine prevent me to enjoy looking at Defeo or Van Gogh?

<figure>
<img src="https://hypersubject.b-cdn.net/images/whitney/rothko-no37-19.jpg" />
<figcaption>Mark Rothko. No. 37/No. 19 (Slate Blue and Brown on Plum), 1958</figcaption>
</figure>

(I gotta admit the plum background in No. 37/No. 19 was such a pleasant color in person. In the picture above it looks like a shitty red.)

I had similar thoughts about Jackson Pollock and almost had the same experience after seeing his Echo Number 25 in MoMA. But in Met, I had the chance to see the new Pollock&Krasner show in which you can see the development of both artists together from the time when they were complete strangers to each other, to marriage and breakup. That show made me appreciate Pollock and his abstract paintings more. What I'm trying to say is maybe after seeing a Rothko show that's curated with care would make me appreciate him more.

## The Met

Metropolitan Museum of Art, the Louvre of North America, has such a vast collection that it's humanly impossible to see everything it has to offer in a single day. We visited the Met on the day of midterm elections and spent almost the whole day there. We were thinking of picking 3-4 wings to see and skipping the rest at the beginning but each wing has an aura of its own, once you catch the glimpse of it you want more.

The highlight of the collection—for me—was August Rodin. Seeing Rodin at Museum d'Orsay in Paris in 2025 made me wanna be a sculptor and seeing works of the great master again was great. Some of the pieces in Met, I saw them before: the Sorrow in Museum d'Orsay and the Martyr in Prague National Gallery. Maybe these pieces were casted into multiple editions.

I think the moment Rodin clicked on me was when I realized how he used the raw stone to create a contrast. I saw this in Paris for the afirst time in the bust of his wife:


This is something I noticed in his stone works of course. Stone has its own texture and form. Clay on the other hand, in its raw form, is a shapeless blob.

##  Guggenheim

In the evening of election day, Guggenheim was offering 1.5 hours of access to their collection by paying as little as 1 dollar per person. At first I was nervous about the short duration but it turned out more than enough.

We started from the top floor because I heard the building, which by the way is piece of art on its own, is designed to be a descension. At the top we saw a sign pointing to Yukoi Kusoma's Infinity Room. After waiting in the line for a few minutes, we went in. It was a room with mirrors on every surface and many led balls dimly shining. Rest in peace to Kusoma, but I found it dull. Once the allocated time (~1 minute) has passed I was simply happy because we didn't waste more time with that.

After leaving the Infinity Room, we had the most pleasant surprise and bumped into Catellan's Comedian. I was completely unaware that it's still on display at Guggenheim. Seeing that banana perfectly placed on the wall and imagining the museum staff periodically replacing the banana as Catellan instructed made me giggle inside and appreciate the Comedian more.

We descended through each rotunda displaying the museum's Pop Collection that includes art from 1960 to today. I found them not very engaging. The majority was photos and videos and some interactive games here and there. Although at this point I was still thinking we're very short on time. If I knew Tannhauser collection was that small, I'd spend more time looking at the Pop pieces. 

On the second floor, we entered the Tannhauser collection. As I said, the collection is small but it has zest. Especially the paintings of Kandinsky were great to see.
