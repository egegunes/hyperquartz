I feel an immense sadness. I'm grieving the loss of a pillar of my self-identity: my career as a software developer. Although I'm not sure if this grief is rational, or whether software development is really doomed for good. But it's clear that a shift has happened, both in the industry and in me.

Using generative AI for software development was not something I took seriously until 2026. All models I used prior to this year were at best naive and at worst deceptive and harmful. However, there's a reason the whole industry echoes the same thing: something changed in January 2026.

It was February when I decided to give Claude a try. The reason was not technical but based on fear. I was vocally against using LLMs for coding at my `$work` and I was afraid that I'd be the first one to be let go as more and more managers started to push people to "leverage" AI in their day-to-day work. As a technical leader in my team, I decided to align myself with the company's wants and approach the problem with an open mind. I knew that I was tangling myself in a [Molochian trap](https://slatestarcodex.com/2014/07/30/meditations-on-moloch/). "I knew what I was doing, I did it anyway."

The more I used an agent to generate code, the more I became dependent on it. It made me lazier in execution and at the same time more ambitious in scoping. The bar for "good enough" has risen while the timeline to achieve it has shrunk. I don't know what hit me recently but this week I realized the same shift that I saw in the industry in January has happened in me. Today I sadly confess that I think using AI agents to write code is integral to my work.

I feel embarrassed to admit this. I want to justify myself to you, anonymous reader, by saying that "I don't enjoy this but it's necessary for my survival because I have bills to pay and people to provide for." I know this is how Moloch speaks. I know that I'm tangled in the Molochian trap.

My work was the primary source of satisfaction for the last 10 years. I loved building things, I liked working with others to achieve something great. Now the work feels alienating. The code that I only wrote 10% of—where I understand 90% of what's going on, knowing the remaining 10% is the most important part—makes me nauseous. I feel like a phony pretending to be an engineer.

Writing this made me remember a blog post that I wrote at the end of 2017 on ["adaptation and self-identification"](https://bengidoom.com/posts/adaptasyon-ve-kendini-tanimlamak-uzerine). It was in Turkish but here's the translation of the crucial part:

> *I'm someone who works in a small software agency and makes a living by developing web applications using Python/Django.* When I define myself this way, any change in those things forces me to change against my will.
> 
> Let's try again: *I enjoy systems governing themselves autonomously and working with each other in harmony.* The computer is the most accessible platform to build such systems.
